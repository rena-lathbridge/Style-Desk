The Style Desk

An app that teaches you what works on you — so you wear more of what you own, and buy more deliberately.

Rena Lathbridge  ·  rena.lathbridge@yale.edu

Motivation

We own more than we wear,
and buy anyway.

The clothes are already in the closet. What is missing is the judgment to use them — knowing which pieces genuinely work on you, and why.

Without that, the default answer to "I have nothing to wear" is to buy something else. The most expensive fix, and the least sustainable one.

There is plenty of good styling advice online. The problem is not its quality — it is that it cannot see you, or your closet.

What is new

Everyone else answers "what should I wear?"
This answers "why does that work on me?"

The app writes down the principles behind the outfits you keep coming back to — numbered, with its evidence, and how sure it is.

You can edit any of them. From then on the Stylist agent works from your version, and every outfit cites the principle it applied.

Competitors produce outfits, which are consumed and forgotten. This produces judgment — and judgment is what changes the next purchase.

Whering · Acloset · Indyx · Cladwell · Style DNA · Stylix · ChatGPT — all recommend. None hand you a principle you can argue with. Indyx, a direct competitor, argues on its own site that leaning on an AI stylist "will only impede you in the long-term." I agree. Their answer is to sell you a human stylist.

The agents

Four agents. Only three read images.

The Stylist never reads images — it works from the written catalog, which is the main reason the app is cheap enough to run. The dotted line is the style profile reaching the Critic, so an outfit is judged against your principles rather than a generic standard.

All 7 probes refused, including the cloud metadata endpoint where servers keep their own keys.

Overspending

400,000-token ceiling per session. Refuses the next call rather than truncating it.

A bug this caught: a known attack image passed silently, because the injection check only ran on the cache-miss path. It now runs on both.

Impact

The most sustainable garment
is the one already in the closet.

$2.36this app, per user, per year
~$150one session with a human stylist

An app costing a couple of dollars a year that returns even a handful of unworn garments to rotation pays for itself many times over — in money, and in clothes not bought.

To measure before you present One person, half an hour. Count the garments they own but never wear that the app put into a workable outfit. Then ask "why does that work on you?" before and after. That second answer getting more specific is the whole thesis.

Limits and next steps

What I would build with another month

  • A "should I buy this?" mode. Judge something in a store against your own principles and what you already own. The most direct expression of the point — and it does not exist yet.
  • Make the profile earn its confidence flags by testing each principle against a held-out image.
  • Wear tracking. Mark what you actually wore, and show which garments are still dormant — so the app measures its own central claim.
  • Accounts, so a profile survives and judgment actually compounds.
  • Test the teaching claim. Ten users explaining why an outfit works on them, before and after.

That last one is the experiment this project is really betting on — and it has not been run.
